# Envy Stir

An Alternate Australian Political Fiction

## Prologue

### CHAPTER 0

Vɪᴄᴛᴏʀʏ always goes to the strong. Exoneration came only when the populace’s thirst for scandal had been parched by a lack of witnesses; Richard Chiang was returned to the ballot not by truth, but by the sheer exhaustion of the mob. It so happens that Elizabeth makes a switch of vote to another candidate for Premier while Richard just goes home alone.

It is the final, quiet erosion. Richard now has a feeling that the entry of new incumbents into the fray upended the delicate equilibrium, for the Premier has the final say in all mandates throughout the country. With a concerned look, Elizabeth offers to pay a compliment to Richard to dispel misgivings.

‘ – How I envy you your talents!’

‘ – Careful, Elizabeth. Your admiration is starting to feel like a eulogy.’

‘What do you mean by saying that?’

‘You've switched your vote, haven’t you?’

‘I am sorry; I didn’t mean to hurt your feelings … Don’t confuse political necessity with a personal vendetta, Dicky …’

‘As a tidy conclusion to your calculus, there won’t be a ray of hope left at this rate!’ Spasm seizes Richard’s wrist.

‘So you blame it on me? Your martyrdom is exhausting, Richard. Must you always bleed for the unwashed masses with such theatrical zeal? You just give out all your capital and vitality to the rabble – the f\*\*\*ing poor!’

‘ … poverty-stricken ones … the marginalised … the disenfranchised … the very people you’ve learned to edit out of your life.’ Richard’s voice now a low, jagged rasping; the pain in his wrist now a involuntary, frantic throbbing ‘None of your nonsense! Spare me your pragmatism. I’ll navigate the wreckage alone with my own hands. Now get out!’

## Gilding the Statue

### Chapter 1: The Fabricator

Iɴ the penthouse of Sydney, the viscous air smelled of the scent of sea and high-interest rates. Richard is there, adjusting his beige linen jacket – $630, a month’s rent in the attic of his father’s shop – the damn fabric still act like he has slept in it – instead, he has ironed it three times this morning. Yet every move creates new stubborn creases marking him as an amateur in the room full of smooth wool and silk.

Richard is speaking, or rather, showcasing a curated artifice of himself to the interstitial between two venture capitalists.

‘The infrastructure for the API is already semi-automated,’ Richard announces, with his Cultivated Australian accent hitting consonants precisely. He has spent his years listening to tapes to scrub his roots out of his vowels, but now, his accent does not sound like those recordings except for loudness under the crystal chandeliers.

The two men don’t even deign to look at him. They slightly shift, performing a subtle but deliberate move that successfully excises Richard from their circle. He was left alone holding a glass of Hunter Valley Semillon with its lean acidity. It’s sharp.

He has woven too many subterfuge this night. About funds that do not exist. About connections that he has never met.

Now, the stories have become a wall trapping him on the outside.

His right wrist is throbbing. The tendonitis, the souvenir from the months spent on all-night back-to-back dev cycles, is flaring up in the viscous air. He tries to hide the trembling hand into his pocket, only furthering his discomfort.

‘Your tie was crooked.’

The voice is cold and tempered, leading Richard to perform a pivot that forces another crease to scream on the shoulder of his jacket.

She is half a step away, exactly a distance inviting a critical audit. She isn’t in a cocktail dress; instead, she wears an understated navy knit that seems to predate the concept of trends. The RP accent lacerates the party.

‘I’m sorry?’ Richard stammers with his polished accent finally faltering.

‘The roadshow video,’ she continues without bothering to reply as her eyes scanning his face with the colour of the grey London mornings, ‘I’ve seen it seven times. People think you’re a fabricator, Richard. They dismiss you as a man of cheap linen and expensive lies.’

The throb in his wrist migrates to his chest. From his chest he scrambles for a rebuttal, a new story to paper over the old cracks.

‘But,’ she explains, and for the first time, he sees the smile: an unspoken smile he won’t comprehend for decades. ‘I have seen the code underneath the stories which shows that your logic is sound.’

‘You have the talents to build structures where others can only see chaos.’

It has been six years since Richard first felt the dull ache in his wrist. It is replaced by a surge of heat that is, he considers, for hope. 

She gazes him, not squinting a liar, but watching a rough-cut masterpiece waiting to be carved. ‘I’m Elizabeth Brown,’ she reaches out, ‘I have a use for a man who builds structures out of entropy. And I think, Richard, you have a use for me.’

‘Stop those cut-rate fabrications. They are beneath you.’ Her tone laces with a sliver of unbidden mercy. ‘You won’t be alone any more. At least I’ll be with you.’ Her eyes stay sharp regardless of a burning blossom rising up.

Richard Chiang smiles.

Excited – Eager –

– Exposed.
